Workout guide: intended zones and purpose

Targets update with FTP, resting HR and maximum HR above

Power zones below use the common seven-zone FTP convention. These are starting ranges, not measured physiological thresholds. Use breathing, repeatability and recovery to refine them; heat, fatigue and short intervals can make heart rate misleading.

Power zones, heart rate and when to adjust

Seven power zones: Z1 <55% FTP (recovery); Z2 55–75% (endurance); Z3 76–90% (tempo); Z4 91–105% (threshold); Z5 106–120% (VO₂/high aerobic); Z6 121–150% (anaerobic capacity); Z7 >150% (neuromuscular). Device boundaries can differ slightly. The medium 3×20 recipe uses tempo Z3: keep all 60 work minutes controlled rather than treating them as threshold intervals.

Heart-rate reserve (HRR): HRR = maximum HR − resting HR. Target bpm = resting HR + intensity fraction × HRR. Easy and tempo ranges are approximate starting guides, not measured threshold boundaries. Use full-sentence conversation for easy riding, controlled short phrases for tempo, and repeatability for hard intervals. The Norwegian target is converted from NTNU's 85–95% maximum-HR guidance into its equivalent HRR percentages with your inputs; it is not 85–95% HRR. Aim for the hard range in the later 2 minutes of each rep, allowing HR to rise gradually. During recovery, move easily and let HR fall rather than chasing a precise number. Heat, fatigue and activity differences can shift HR at the same effort.

FTP uncertainty: Garmin's FTP is an estimate; if the 3×20 tempo target feels near maximal early or the 5×4 repeats fade substantially, reduce power and reassess. A field assessment with steady power data can improve these targets. The guide does not affect the optimizer's schedule ranking.

Generating legal two-week cycles and testing 16 plausible models…

Run the optimizer to sample a large structured set of legal 14-day cycles satisfying your locks. A plan is “robust” when it remains in the lowest-conflict decile across many plausible weight combinations.

What the optimizer actually rewards
  • All selected day locks are enforced as hard constraints before schedules are ranked.
  • Exactly 2 Upper, the selected 3 or 4 rides (or alternating 4 and 3), 1 sprint-containing easy ride and one full rest day each week, plus the selected 3- or 4-Lower fortnightly target. Every weekday has at most one session. Doubles are allowed only on weekends.
  • More separation between demanding Lower sessions and fewer medium/hard rides close to Lower. Upper sessions are encouraged toward even, roughly 84-hour gaps across all four sessions in the repeating cycle; 48 hours remains the hard minimum.
  • With two 5×4 rides, roughly even spacing through the repeating 14-day cycle and enough recovery from recent Lower, medium, sprint, or long rides to execute the hard work well. The 5×4 goal increases these weights; it does not estimate VO₂ minutes or prescribe a proven optimal gap.
  • Non-negotiable recovery: the calendar day after each Lower is exclusively Upper or a full rest day, with no cycling of any intensity. The fortnight repeats, including Week 2 Sunday→Week 1 Monday. This is enforced before scoring and when manually editing.
  • Weekend rides may share days with Upper. Lower never shares a day with anything, even easy cycling before Lower. Brief sprints stay attached to short easy rides rather than becoming full interval days. Sprint-containing rides are penalized when close to Lower or another hard ride.
  • Optional avoidance of 8 AM sessions, preference for a weekend rest day, and preference for placing each long easy ride on a weekend. “Require weekend” makes that placement a hard rule.

For each legal sampled cycle, 16 deterministic parameter sets are drawn from deliberately broad ranges. Results report the share of models in which the cycle lands in the best 10% among the sampled cycles satisfying the locks. Penalty units are scheduling heuristics, not physiological measurements. Scores across different Lower-session counts are not comparable: the model does not predict hypertrophy.

Evidence and limits

Primary reviews and studies used to set the rules
  • Concurrent training generally preserves maximal strength and whole-muscle hypertrophy, although explosive strength may be attenuated in the same session: Schumann et al.
  • A small fiber-level hypertrophy effect remains possible, with clearer concern for running than cycling: Lundberg et al.
  • Resistance-exercise glycogen depletion varies with sets, duration, intensity and training status: Hamidvand et al.
  • Carbohydrate during cycling can sometimes spare muscle glycogen, but the magnitude is protocol-dependent: Stellingwerff et al. and Arkinstall et al.
  • With volume equated, resistance-training frequency does not meaningfully determine hypertrophy: Schoenfeld et al.
  • Norwegian heart-rate and recovery guidance: NTNU CERG. The app converts 85–95% maximum HR to HRR and uses five repetitions.
  • Four-minute intervals are a well-supported high-aerobic format, while longer work bouts such as 4×8 minutes can produce comparable or greater adaptations when effort is appropriately controlled: Seiler et al. The planner uses 5×4 as a five-repetition adaptation of Norwegian 4×4, and 3×20 as a controlled tempo recipe. Durations alone do not establish intensity.
  • Brief maximal sprints can be embedded in otherwise easy cycling. Studies have mainly used different doses and elite cyclists; two 4–6 × 10-second sprint rides per fortnight is a personal training preference, not a validated optimal dose: Almquist et al.
  • Endurance intensity-distribution research supports substantial low-intensity volume but does not identify one universally superior distribution for every athlete: Rosenblat et al.
  • Subjective well-being can be more sensitive than common objective monitoring measures: Saw et al.
  • The acute:chronic workload ratio is not used because its causal and statistical basis is unsuitable for training recommendations: Impellizzeri et al.

Not modeled: injury probability, connective-tissue status, exact glycogen, bar velocity, calorie deficit, illness, exercise-specific volume, or individualized response. The optimizer narrows choices; it does not clear an athlete to train.

If repeated Lower performance, interval power, sleep or soreness disagree with the suggested cycle, trust the observations and adjust it. Exact physiological percentages were deliberately removed.
